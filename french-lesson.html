Consonant Rules

Silent final consonants ▼

If a word ends in two consonants, ignore the last one. If a word ends in vowel + consonant, also ignore the consonant.

concert→ [kɔ̃sɛʀ] — the final -t is silent
restaurant→ no -t at the end
petit→ "puh-tee" — the -t is silent
Louis, François, Nicolas→ all silent final consonants

But add -e at the end? Then you DO pronounce the last consonant.

couvert → couvertethe final -t gets pronounced in the feminine
petit → petite"puh-teet" — the -t is pronounced
cerise · fraise · framboisecherry · strawberry · raspberry
The CaReFuL rule ▼

Words ending in C, R, F, L — pronounce these letters.

Daniel · Marc · Éricall final consonants pronounced
neuf · parc · chocnine · park · shock
Exceptions — do NOT pronounce the -c:
blanc, banc, cerf, tabac
-er verbs are always "ay" sound: danser, parler, voyager — the CaReFuL rule does NOT apply to these.
QU = K ▼

"qu" in French always sounds like "k" — not like the English "qu" in "quantity".

question= "kestion"
quoi · quand · quikwa · kã · ki (what · when · who)
quantité= "kantité"
Single -s vs double -ss ▼

Single -s between vowels = sounds like z

poison= "pwa-ZON" (poison)
Isabelle · désert · ciseauxall with z-sound

Double -ss = sounds like s

poisson= "pwa-SON" (fish) — don't mix these up!
dessert · chaussette · messageall with s-sound
Soft & hard C and G ▼

C before e or i = soft "s" sound

cerise · certain · cinémacherry · certain · cinema

C before anything else = hard "k" sound

crêpe · coïncidence · classecrepe · coincidence · class

G before e, i, y = soft "zh" sound (like the "s" in "measure")

géant · girafe · génialgiant · giraffe · great

G before anything else = hard "g"

grand · gros · gorgebig · fat · throat
Special combinations: -gn, -ll, -h, -th, -ph ▼

-gn = "ny" sound (like Spanish ñ or English "canyon")

gagner · signer · cygneto win · to sign · swan

-ll after most vowels = soft "y" sound. Exception: mille, ville, tranquille (and related words) keep a regular -l.

fille · famille · vanillegirl · family · vanilla (y-sound)
million · villagel-sound (related to mille/ville)

-h at the start of a word = completely silent

hôtel · hôpital · histoirehotel · hospital · history

-th = just a "t" (ignore the h)

mathématiques · thé · athlètemaths · tea · athlete

-ph = "f" sound

éléphant · Philippeelephant · Philippe
Tricky endings: -euil, -ouille, -eille, -aille ▼

-euil = "ɵy" sound (very French!)

feuilleleaf → "fuyh"
accueil · écureuil · portefeuillereception · squirrel · wallet

-ouille = "ou-ee" sound

grenouille · nouillefrog · pasta

-eille = "ay" sound; -aille = "eye" sound

Reading Exercise

Read this text aloud, applying all the rules above. Aim for smooth pronunciation.

Au petit-déjeuner, on prend du pain ou des biscottes. Sur le pain, on met du beurre et de la confiture. On appelle ça des tartines. On boit aussi du café au lait, du thé ou du chocolat chaud. On peut aussi prendre un bol de lait avec des céréales. On peut aussi boire un jus de fruits ou manger un yaourt. Le dimanche matin, les Français vont à la boulangerie pour acheter des croissants ou des pains au chocolat.

Translation: For breakfast, we have bread or crackers. On the bread we put butter and jam — we call this "tartines". We also drink café au lait, tea, or hot chocolate. On Sundays, the French go to the bakery to buy croissants or pain au chocolat.

Why is 70 "sixty-ten"? French historically counted in 20s. So 70 = soixante-dix (60+10), 71 = soixante et onze, 72 = soixante-douze… up to 79 = soixante-dix-neuf. Same logic for 90s.

Rule 1 — Use et for numbers ending in 1

Rule 2 — Use - (hyphen) for numbers ending in 3–9

Rule 3 — cents takes an -s only when alone

200 = deux-cents (ends here → add -s)
204 = deux-cent-quatre (followed by more → no -s)

Articles — Le, La, Un, Du…

French has definite articles (the), indefinite articles (a/an), and partitive articles (some). They change based on gender and whether the noun is specific or general.

Simple Memory Trick

Think of it this way:
a / an in English → un / une in French
the in English → le / la / les in French
some in English → du / de la / des in French

When to use each

Definite: le / la / les — "the specific thing"

Use when both speaker and listener know exactly what is being referred to.

Le chat dort sur le sofa.The cat sleeps on the sofa. (a specific cat)
Ma mère aime le chocolat.My mother likes chocolate. (chocolate in general)
Indefinite: un / une — "one of many"

Use for something the listener doesn't know about, one instance of something.

J'ai un ami.I have a friend. (one friend among many)
Il y a des erreurs dans ce texte.There are (some) errors in this text.
Partitive: du / de la — "some quantity of"

For uncountable things or abstract nouns where you don't specify the amount.

Tu veux du chocolat ?Do you want some chocolate?
J'ai de la chance.I'm lucky. (lit: I have some luck)
Je prends des vacances.I'm taking some holiday.
Negatives: always → de / d'

In a negative sentence, all partitive and indefinite articles become de (or d' before a vowel).

J'ai de la chance → Je n'ai pas de chance.I'm lucky → I'm not lucky.
Il y a des erreurs → Il n'y a pas d'erreurs.There are errors → There are no errors.
Exception with être: After être, the article doesn't change in negatives.
C'est du pain → Ce n'est pas du pain. (stays "du")

The key rule: If you say "my car" in French, you don't look at yourself — you look at the word for "car". If voiture is feminine, you say ma voiture.

Grammar

Building Sentences

French sentence structure is very consistent. The word order almost never changes — unlike in English.

Affirmative (positive) sentences

Always: Subject → Verb → Complement

Le chien attrape la balle. — The dog catches the ball.

You can never swap subject and complement in French (in an affirmative sentence).

Two complements? Direct complement first, then indirect:
Morgan montre le chemin (direct) à Sasha (indirect).
Morgan shows the way to Sasha.

Negative sentences

Subject + ne + Verb + pas + Complement
Leo n'a pas faim.Leo is not hungry.
Laura n'est pas gentille.Laura is not nice.
Sasha ne va pas à l'école.Sasha doesn't go to school.

Grammar

Passé Composé

The most common past tense. Formed with a "helper" verb (avoir or être) + the past participle.

The formula

Subject + avoir/être (present) + past participle

J'ai mangé du kangourou. — I ate kangaroo.

Morgan est allée en Tasmanie. — Morgan went to Tasmania.

avoir or être?

Most verbs use avoir. A specific group of verbs use être — mainly verbs of movement and all reflexive verbs. A handy mnemonic: DR. & MRS. VANDERTRAMP (Devenir, Revenir, Monter, Rester, Sortir, Venir, Aller, Naître, Descendre, Entrer, Retourner, Tomber, Rentrer, Arriver, Mourir, Partir).

Grammar

L'Imparfait

The imperfect tense. Used for past habits, ongoing past states, and descriptions of how things were.

When to use it

Ongoing states / descriptions in the past

Il faisait froid, les arbres étaient gelés. — It was cold, the trees were frozen.

Repeated actions or habits in the past

Quand j'étais enfant, j'allais souvent voir les canards. — When I was a child, I often went to see the ducks.

Contrast: past vs now

Aujourd'hui je dors peu, alors qu'avant je dormais beaucoup. — Now I sleep little, whereas before I slept a lot.

How to conjugate

1
Take the verb in the present tense.
2
Conjugate it with nous. Example: venir → nous venons
3
Drop the -ons → ven- (the stem)
4
Add the imperfect endings: -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient

Sample Dialogue

Client
Bonjour, je voudrais une table pour deux, s'il vous plaît.
Hello, a table for two please.
Serveur
Bien sûr, suivez-moi. Vous souhaitez boire quelque chose pour commencer ?
Of course, follow me. Would you like something to drink to start?
Client
Un verre de vin rouge pour moi, et un Perrier pour mon amie.
A glass of red wine for me, and a Perrier for my friend.
Serveur
Et pour commencer, que souhaitez-vous en entrée ?
And for your starter, what would you like?
Client
Pour moi, ce sera la salade de chèvre chaud. Et pour le plat principal, le steak-frites, saignant, s'il vous plaît.
For me, the warm goat's cheese salad. And for the main, steak and fries, medium-rare, please.
Serveur
Parfait. Souhaitez-vous un dessert après le repas ?
Perfect. Would you like a dessert after the meal?
Client
Oui, une crème brûlée, s'il vous plaît. Et l'addition quand vous pouvez.
Yes, a crème brûlée please. And the bill when you can.
